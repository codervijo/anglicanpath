## Anglican vs Catholic: the short answer

Anglicans and Roman Catholics hold most of the Christian faith in common. Both
confess the Trinity and the Incarnation, both recite the Nicene Creed, both
baptise, both celebrate the Eucharist, and both are governed by bishops who
claim a ministry handed down from the apostles. A visitor who walked into a
traditional parish of either kind would find a structured liturgy, a church
year, set readings, and a priest at an altar.

The division is narrower than that shared ground and harder to bridge. It
concerns where final authority in the Church rests. Roman Catholics hold that
the Bishop of Rome, as successor of Peter, exercises a universal jurisdiction
over the whole Church and can in defined circumstances teach without error.
Anglicans do not. That single disagreement carries the rest with it: what
counts as binding doctrine, who may settle a disputed question, and whether one
Church can recognise the other's ministry.

If you are new to the tradition, it is worth reading
[what the Anglican Church actually is](/learn/what-is-the-anglican-church/)
before working through the comparison below, because much of what follows
assumes you know what Anglicans are claiming for themselves.

## Anglican Church vs Catholic Church: authority and the papacy

Ask a Roman Catholic where the Church's teaching authority lies and the answer
is reasonably clear: in the college of bishops in communion with the Pope, and
in the Pope himself when he defines a doctrine of faith or morals for the whole
Church. That authority is living and can speak again tomorrow.

Ask an Anglican and the answer is more layered, and Anglicans differ among
themselves about how to weight the layers. Scripture is supreme; nothing may be
required of anyone as necessary to salvation that cannot be proved from it. The
creeds and the ancient ecumenical councils are received as faithful summaries of
what Scripture teaches. Beyond that, Anglicans appeal to the historic
formularies — the Book of Common Prayer, the Thirty-Nine Articles and the
Ordinal — as the documents that give Anglicanism its particular shape.

The practical difference is this. When Rome defines a doctrine, the question is
closed for Roman Catholics. Anglicanism has no equivalent mechanism, which
critics call incoherence and defenders call restraint: the claim is that the
Church may not bind consciences beyond what Scripture and the undivided Church
have already settled. Whichever way you read it, it explains why Anglican
provinces can disagree publicly in a way that would be structurally impossible
in the Roman communion.

Two Roman definitions in particular mark the boundary, because neither has any
Anglican equivalent and both were made after the separation: the dogma of papal
infallibility, and the two Marian dogmas of the Immaculate Conception and the
Assumption.

## Is Anglican Protestant?

Both yes and no, and the honest answer has to say why the question is harder
than it looks.

Yes, in that the Church of England separated from Rome in the sixteenth century
and was reformed under the influence of the continental Reformation. Its
formularies were written in that period and bear its marks: the supremacy of
Scripture, justification by faith, worship and Bible in the vernacular, a
married clergy, communion in both kinds for the laity.

No, in that Anglicanism did not understand itself as founding a new church. It
kept the threefold ministry of bishops, priests and deacons; it kept the creeds,
the church year and the sacraments; it kept a liturgy that is in large part
translated and edited from the medieval Latin rites rather than composed afresh.
Its claim was to be the ancient Church in England, reformed — not a new body
gathered around a new confession.

Which half of that answer an Anglican emphasises depends heavily on
churchmanship. An Evangelical Anglican will tell you plainly that the Church of
England is a Protestant church. An Anglo-Catholic will say it is a part of the
Western Catholic Church that happens to be in a state of separation from Rome.
Both are speaking in good faith, and both can point to the formularies. This is
not evasion; it is a real and long-standing internal disagreement, and you will
meet it in the parishes.

## Catholic vs Anglican on the sacraments

Here the two are closer than most readers expect, and the differences are
specific rather than sweeping.

Rome numbers seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance,
anointing of the sick, holy orders and matrimony. The Anglican formularies
distinguish between two sacraments "ordained of Christ in the Gospel" — baptism
and the Supper of the Lord — and five others which they describe as commonly
called sacraments but treat as standing on a different footing.

In practice, most Anglican parishes offer all seven in some form. The
distinction is about what may be required of everyone as necessary, not about
what is available or valued.

### The Eucharist

Both traditions hold that Christ is truly present in the Eucharist and that it
is more than a memorial. They divide on how that presence is explained and on
whether any explanation may be made binding.

Roman Catholic teaching uses the term transubstantiation. Anglican teaching
rejects transubstantiation as a required explanation while affirming the real
presence — and Anglicans have then disagreed among themselves for four
centuries about what to put in its place. Positions across Anglicanism range
from a receptionist view, in which the faithful receive Christ by faith in the
act of communion, to views practically indistinguishable from the Roman one.

A visitor should know that this range is visible. In one parish the priest may
genuflect and the reserved sacrament may be kept in an aumbry with a lamp; in
another, a few miles away, neither will be true.

### Confession, marriage and holy orders

**Confession.** Rome requires the confession of grave sin to a priest.
Anglicanism provides the same rite and commends it, but does not oblige it — the
traditional Anglican formulation is that all may, none must, some should.

**Marriage.** Rome treats a valid consummated marriage between the baptised as
indissoluble, with annulment the only remedy. Anglican provinces vary and
generally permit remarriage after divorce under conditions set locally, which is
one of the more practically significant differences for anyone in a second
marriage.

**Holy orders.** This is the sharpest point. Rome has formally judged Anglican
ordinations invalid, and that judgement has never been withdrawn. Several
Anglican provinces now ordain women, which Rome does not, and which has also
divided Anglicans from one another.

## Anglican Catholic vs Roman Catholic: what "Anglo-Catholic" means

Readers searching this phrase are usually after one of two different things.

An **Anglo-Catholic** is an Anglican of a particular churchmanship. The movement
began in nineteenth-century Oxford as an argument that the Church of England was
a branch of the Catholic Church rather than a Protestant denomination, and it
recovered much that the Reformation had set aside: vestments, incense, the
daily Mass, auricular confession, religious orders. An Anglo-Catholic parish can
look and sound very like a Roman Catholic one. It is not in communion with Rome,
and the people in it are Anglicans.

The **Anglican Catholic Church**, by contrast, is the proper name of a specific
jurisdiction — one of the Continuing Anglican bodies formed in North America in
the later twentieth century. It is a church, not a churchmanship. If a parish
near you uses that exact name, it belongs to that jurisdiction.

So: Anglo-Catholic describes how a parish worships and what it believes about
itself. Anglican Catholic, capitalised and as a proper name, tells you which
church it belongs to. The two often overlap, and confusing them will send you to
the wrong building on a Sunday morning.

## Church of England vs Catholic Church: how the split happened

The popular account — that Henry VIII wanted a divorce and invented a church to
get one — is memorable, incomplete, and misleading about what actually resulted.

The break in jurisdiction was indeed royal and political, and it was accomplished
by act of Parliament rather than by theological argument. But the Church that
emerged over the following century and a half was shaped far more by what came
after: two vernacular prayer books under Edward VI, a return to Rome under Mary,
the settlement under Elizabeth I that fixed the shape of English worship, the
Articles that fixed its doctrinal boundaries, and after the upheavals of the
seventeenth century the 1662 Book of Common Prayer, which remains the Church of
England's standard to this day.

Two things follow that matter for a reader comparing the two churches today.
First, what Anglicans actually believe was settled by those documents, not by
Henry's marital circumstances — so judging Anglicanism by its origin story is a
category mistake. Second, Rome did not stand still either; the Council of Trent,
and much later the First and Second Vatican Councils, shaped the Roman Catholic
Church a reader meets today just as decisively.

If you want to see the Anglican side of that settlement for yourself rather than
take anyone's summary of it, the place to start is
[the 1662 Book of Common Prayer](/learn/1662-book-of-common-prayer/).

## Which should I visit?

If the question of authority is the one that presses on you — if you want a
Church that can say definitively what must be believed, and you find that
prospect a relief rather than a constraint — then Rome is answering the question
you are actually asking, and an Anglican parish will frustrate you.

If what draws you is the liturgy, the prayer book, the daily office and a
tradition that holds the ancient faith while leaving certain questions open,
and if you can live with the fact that Anglicans disagree in public, then a
traditional Anglican parish is likely to be a home rather than a compromise.

Neither decision is made well in the abstract. Read what each church says in its
own documents rather than what the other says about it, and then go to both on a
Sunday. If you have not been before, it is worth knowing
[what to expect at an Anglican service](/learn/first-anglican-service-what-to-expect/)
so the practicalities do not get in the way of the visit.
