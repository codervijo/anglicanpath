## Anglican vs Catholic: the short answer

Anglicans and Roman Catholics hold most of the Christian faith in common. Both
confess the Trinity and the Incarnation, both recite the Nicene Creed, both
baptise, both celebrate the Eucharist, and both are governed by bishops who
claim a ministry handed down from the apostles. A visitor to a traditional
parish of either kind would find a structured liturgy, a church year, set
readings, and a priest at an altar.

The division is narrower than that shared ground and harder to bridge. It
concerns where final authority in the Church rests. Roman Catholics hold that
the Bishop of Rome, as successor of Peter, exercises universal jurisdiction over
the whole Church and can in defined circumstances teach without error. Anglicans
deny both claims. Everything else follows from that: what counts as binding
doctrine, who may settle a disputed question, and whether either church can
recognise the other's ministry.

## Anglican Church vs Catholic Church: authority and the papacy

Ask a Roman Catholic where teaching authority lies and the answer is clear: in
the bishops in communion with the Pope, and in the Pope himself when he defines
a doctrine of faith or morals for the whole Church. That authority is living and
can speak again tomorrow.

The Anglican answer is layered, and Anglicans weight the layers differently.
Scripture is supreme. Article VI of the Thirty-nine Articles puts the limit
plainly:

> Holy Scripture containeth all things necessary to salvation: so that
> whatsoever is not read therein, nor may be proved thereby, is not to be
> required of any man, that it should be believed as an article of the Faith, or
> be thought requisite or necessary to salvation.

The creeds and the ancient councils are received as faithful summaries of what
Scripture teaches. Beyond that, Anglicans appeal to the historic formularies —
the Book of Common Prayer, the Thirty-nine Articles and the Ordinal.

On Rome's jurisdiction the Articles are blunt. Article XXXVII states: *"The
Bishop of Rome hath no jurisdiction in this Realm of England."*

The practical difference is this. When Rome defines a doctrine, the question
closes. Anglicanism has no equivalent mechanism — critics call that incoherence,
defenders call it restraint, the claim being that the Church may not bind
consciences beyond what Scripture and the undivided Church have already settled.
Either way it explains why Anglican provinces disagree in public in a way that
would be structurally impossible in the Roman communion.

Three Roman definitions mark the boundary most sharply, because none has an
Anglican equivalent and all three were made long after the separation: papal
infallibility, defined by the First Vatican Council in *Pastor Aeternus* on 18
July 1870; the Immaculate Conception, defined by Pius IX in *Ineffabilis Deus*
on 8 December 1854; and the Assumption, defined by Pius XII in
*Munificentissimus Deus* on 1 November 1950. An Anglican is not required to hold
any of them, because none can be proved from Scripture in the sense Article VI
demands.

## Is Anglican Protestant?

Both yes and no, and the honest answer has to say why the question is harder
than it looks.

Yes, in that the Church of England separated from Rome in the sixteenth century
and was reformed under the influence of the continental Reformation. Its
formularies come from that period and bear its marks: the supremacy of
Scripture, justification by faith, worship and Bible in the vernacular, a married
clergy, communion in both kinds for the laity.

No, in that Anglicanism did not understand itself as founding a new church. It
kept the threefold ministry of bishops, priests and deacons; it kept the creeds,
the church year and the sacraments; and its liturgy is in large part translated
and edited from the medieval Latin rites rather than composed afresh. The claim
was to be the ancient Church in England, reformed — not a new body gathered
around a new confession.

Which half an Anglican emphasises depends heavily on churchmanship. An
Evangelical Anglican will tell you plainly that the Church of England is a
Protestant church. An Anglo-Catholic will say it is part of the Western Catholic
Church that happens to be separated from Rome. Both are speaking in good faith
and both can cite the formularies. This is not evasion; it is a real and
long-standing internal disagreement, and you will meet it in the parishes.

## Catholic vs Anglican on the sacraments

Here the two are closer than most readers expect, and the differences are
specific rather than sweeping.

Rome numbers seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance,
anointing of the sick, holy orders and matrimony. Article XXV draws a line
through that list:

> There are two Sacraments ordained of Christ our Lord in the Gospel, that is to
> say, Baptism, and the Supper of the Lord.

The other five it calls *"those five commonly called Sacraments, that is to say,
Confirmation, Penance, Orders, Matrimony, and Extreme Unction"* — and says they
*"are not to be counted for Sacraments of the Gospel."*

That is sharper than modern Anglican practice. Most Anglican parishes today
offer all seven in some form, and an Anglo-Catholic parish will speak of seven
without hesitation. The Article's distinction is about what may be required of
everyone as necessary to salvation, not about what is available or valued.

### The Eucharist

Both traditions hold that Christ is truly present in the Eucharist and that it is
more than a memorial. They divide on how that presence is explained and on
whether any explanation may be made binding.

Roman Catholic teaching uses the term transubstantiation. Article XXVIII rejects
it as a required explanation:

> Transubstantiation (or the change of the substance of Bread and Wine) in the
> Supper of the Lord, cannot be proved by Holy Writ; but is repugnant to the
> plain words of Scripture, overthroweth the nature of a Sacrament, and hath
> given occasion to many superstitions.

The same Article affirms a real receiving: the Body of Christ is *"given, taken,
and eaten, in the Supper, only after an heavenly and spiritual manner,"* and the
means of receiving is faith. Anglicans have then disagreed among themselves for
four centuries about what to put in transubstantiation's place, from a
receptionist view to positions practically indistinguishable from the Roman one.

Article XXXI goes further, calling the *"sacrifices of Masses, in the which it
was commonly said, that the Priest did offer Christ for the quick and the dead,
to have remission of pain or guilt"* — *"blasphemous fables, and dangerous
deceits."* Read it as sixteenth-century polemic against a specific late-medieval
practice rather than as a description of what a Roman Catholic priest believes
he is doing at an altar today. Anglican and Roman Catholic theologians have
spent decades in formal dialogue narrowing exactly this question.

A visitor should know the range is visible. In one parish the priest may
genuflect and the sacrament may be reserved in an aumbry with a lamp; a few
miles away, neither will be true.

### Confession, marriage and holy orders

**Confession.** Rome requires the confession of grave sin to a priest.
Anglicanism provides the same rite and commends it but does not oblige it — the
traditional formulation is that all may, none must, some should.

**Marriage.** Rome treats a valid consummated marriage between the baptised as
indissoluble, with annulment the only remedy. Anglican provinces vary and
generally permit remarriage after divorce under locally set conditions, which is
one of the more practically significant differences for anyone in a second
marriage.

**Holy orders.** This is the sharpest point. In *Apostolicae Curae*, dated 13
September 1896, Leo XIII declared Anglican ordinations "absolutely null and
utterly void," and that judgement has never been withdrawn. The Archbishops of
Canterbury and York, Frederick Temple and William Maclagan, replied on 19
February 1897 in *Saepius Officio*. Several Anglican provinces now also ordain
women, which Rome does not, and which divides Anglicans from one another as well.

## Anglican Catholic vs Roman Catholic: what "Anglo-Catholic" means

Readers searching this phrase usually want one of two different things.

An **Anglo-Catholic** is an Anglican of a particular churchmanship. The movement
began with John Keble's assize sermon, *National Apostasy*, preached at St Mary
the Virgin, Oxford, on 14 July 1833, and was carried forward by the *Tracts for
the Times*. Its argument was that the Church of England is a branch of the
Catholic Church rather than a Protestant denomination, and it recovered much the
Reformation had set aside: vestments, incense, the daily Mass, auricular
confession, religious orders. An Anglo-Catholic parish can look and sound very
like a Roman Catholic one. It is not in communion with Rome, and the people in
it are Anglicans.

The **Anglican Catholic Church**, by contrast, is the proper name of a specific
jurisdiction — one of the Continuing Anglican bodies that grew out of the
Congress of St Louis, held 14–16 September 1977, which produced the Affirmation
of St Louis. It is a church, not a churchmanship. If a parish near you uses that
exact name, that is the body it belongs to.

So: Anglo-Catholic describes how a parish worships and what it believes about
itself. Anglican Catholic, capitalised and as a proper name, tells you which
church it belongs to. The two often overlap, and confusing them will send you to
the wrong building on a Sunday morning.

## Church of England vs Catholic Church: how the split happened

The popular account — that Henry VIII wanted a divorce and invented a church to
get one — is memorable, incomplete, and misleading about what resulted.

The break in jurisdiction was royal and political, accomplished by act of
Parliament rather than by theological argument: the Act of Supremacy of 1534
declared the king supreme head of the Church of England. But the Church that
emerged was shaped far more by what came after. Two vernacular prayer books
appeared under Edward VI, in 1549 and 1552. Mary I restored communion with Rome.
The Elizabethan Settlement of 1559 fixed the shape of English worship with a new
Act of Supremacy, an Act of Uniformity and a further prayer book. The
Thirty-nine Articles reached their final form in 1571. After the upheavals of
the seventeenth century, the Act of Uniformity of 1662 annexed the Book of
Common Prayer that remains the Church of England's standard today.

Two things follow for a reader comparing the two churches now. First, what
Anglicans believe was settled by those documents, not by Henry's marital
circumstances, so judging Anglicanism by its origin story is a category mistake.
Second, Rome did not stand still either: the Council of Trent sat from 1545 to
1563, largely in response to the Reformation, and the Second Vatican Council of
1962 to 1965 reshaped Roman Catholic worship and self-understanding into the
form a visitor meets today. Neither church is the one that separated.

## Which should I visit?

If the question of authority is the one that presses on you — if you want a
church that can say definitively what must be believed, and you find that
prospect a relief rather than a constraint — then Rome is answering the question
you are actually asking, and an Anglican parish will frustrate you.

If what draws you is the liturgy, the prayer book, the daily office and a
tradition that holds the ancient faith while leaving certain questions open, and
you can live with Anglicans disagreeing in public, then a traditional Anglican
parish is likely to be a home rather than a compromise.

Neither decision is made well in the abstract. Read what each church says in its
own documents rather than what the other says about it — the Articles quoted
above take twenty minutes — and then go to both on a Sunday. If you want a route
through the questions rather than a single page, the
[guided reading paths](/paths/) are ordered for readers coming from a particular
background, and the rest of the [library](/learn/) covers the prayer book, the
jurisdictions and what happens at a service. You can also
[pray the daily office](/office/) as Anglicans do, from the 1662 or 1928 book,
before you decide anything.
